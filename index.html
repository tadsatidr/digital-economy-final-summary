Digital Banking & Digital Currency

Source: Digital Banking and Digital Currency (24 slides, 2-hour session). Digital banking changes how money is accessed; digital currency changes what money is.

Core idea: The deck's one-line summary: digital banking changes how money is accessed; digital currency changes what money is. Regulation and trust decide how fast both grow.

1. Digital banking

Meaning: delivering banking products (accounts, payments, loans, savings, investment) through online and mobile channels, with little or no need to visit a branch. (ธนาคารดิจิทัล = ช่องทางให้บริการ)

Internet banking

A traditional bank offers its services through a website.

Mobile banking

App-based services: transfers, QR payments, bill payment, card controls.

Digital-only bank

No branches. The app is the bank (neobanks and virtual banks).

Meaning: money that exists only in electronic form and is recorded on a digital ledger. Digital banking is a channel for money; digital currency is a form of money.

How digital currency works (5 steps)

  1. WalletUser holds keys or an account that proves ownership.
  2. TransactionSender signs a payment instruction.
  3. ValidationNetwork or central operator checks it.
  4. LedgerPayment is recorded on a blockchain or central ledger.
  5. SettlementFunds are final and visible to the receiver.

Cryptocurrency

Meaning: a digital asset secured by cryptography and recorded on a blockchain run by a distributed network, with no central issuer such as a bank or government.

Blockchain

A shared ledger copied across many computers, so no single party controls the record.

Cryptography

Private keys prove ownership and digital signatures authorise each payment.

Consensus

Proof of work or proof of stake lets the network agree which transactions are valid.

Redeemable

Likely exam questions

Short Distinguish digital banking from digital currency.
  • Digital banking = channel to deliver bank services (accounts, payments, loans) online/mobile; stands behind it a licensed bank; money is fiat deposits; safety net = deposit insurance and supervision.
  • Digital currency = form of money recorded on a digital ledger; backed by a network, private issuer or central bank; safety net varies.
  • Mention convergence (banks offering crypto custody; CBDC via bank apps).
Short Name and explain the three types of digital currency, with one example each.
  • Cryptocurrency – decentralised network, value from supply and demand, e.g. Bitcoin.
  • Stablecoin – private company, reserves and peg, e.g. USDC.
  • CBDC – central bank, legal tender, e.g. e-CNY or Sand Dollar.
Short List the five steps of how a digital currency payment works.

Wallet → Transaction (signed) → Validation (network or central operator) → Ledger (blockchain or central ledger) → Settlement (final and visible to receiver).

Short What are the three types of stablecoin and their main risks?

  • Crypto-backed (DAI): collateral price crash.
  • Algorithmic (TerraUSD, collapsed 2022): loss of peg, death spiral.

Smart Industry

Source: Introduction to Transformation to Smart Industry (Part 1 and Part 2, 32 slides). Concepts, impact on manufacturing, examples and use cases.

Definition: Smart industry = manufacturing that connects machines, people and data so production can sense, decide and adapt in near real time. Also called Industry 4.0. (โรงงานอัจฉริยะ)

1. Four industrial revolutions

Each wave raised productivity. Industry 4.0 adds data and connectivity, so machines and products can exchange information.

StageWhenKey ideaTechnology
1.0Late 1700sMechanizationSteam and water power in factories
2.0Early 1900sMass productionElectricity and assembly lines
3.01970sAutomationComputers and programmable controllers
4.02010s onwardSmart industryIoT, data and AI link the whole factory

2. Core technologies

IoT and sensors

Machines report temperature, vibration and output continuously.

Big data and analytics

Turns shop-floor data into decisions on cost, quality and demand.

AI and machine learning

Predicts failures, detects defects and optimizes schedules.

Cloud and edge computing

Stores and processes data at scale and close to the machine.

Digital twins and 3D printing

Test designs virtually and make small batches cheaply.

Robotics and cobots

Flexible automation that works beside people.

3. Impact on manufacturing: traditional vs smart factory

4. Smart manufacturing: Connect → Analyze → Act

  1. ConnectSensors and networks collect data from machines, products and workers.
  2. AnalyzeAnalytics and AI find patterns, predict problems and suggest actions.
  3. ActSystems adjust machines, schedules and orders automatically or with human approval.

How the systems fit together (data flows up, decisions flow down):

Implementation roadmap (6 steps)

  1. Set goalsLink the investment to a business target such as cost, quality or lead time.
  2. Assess and pilotCheck current systems and start with one line or one problem.
  3. Connect dataInstall sensors and integrate machine and enterprise systems.
  4. Analyze and automateUse dashboards, AI models and automated controls on the pilot.
  5. Scale upCopy proven solutions to other lines and plants.
  6. Develop peopleTrain staff and manage the change in roles and routines.

The economics of investing

The deck's illustrative curve (not real data): cumulative net benefit first dips during the investment phase (sensors, software, training), then rises in the payback-and-gains phase as productivity and cost savings accumulate. Smart manufacturing needs upfront capital, then pays back.

5. Case studies

Siemens Electronics Works Amberg (Germany)

Opened in 1989 to make Simatic industrial controllers; often shown as a model digital factory. (Company-reported figures: Siemens, Automation World, Control Engineering. The deck says to verify company figures before citing.)

1 product / secondproduction pace
75%of the production process is automated
99.9988%built-in quality rate reported by Siemens
350product changeovers a day across about 1,200 products
  • Output grew about eight-fold in the same floor area since 1989.
  • Employee ideas account for around 40% of yearly productivity gains.
  • Smart because the product tells the machine what to do next, so one line can handle many variants.
  • Economic lens: capital and data raise output per worker while people move to supervising and improving. Class question: does automation like this reduce jobs, or change what jobs are?

Haier COSMOPlat (China)

  • An industrial internet platform launched by Haier that connects users, suppliers and factories.
  • Customers help design products, so production starts from real demand instead of forecasts.
  • Economic lens: lower inventory risk and cheaper variety; a shift from selling units to serving users.

Thailand 4.0 and the EEC

Low-cost production→Smart manufacturing→Higher value and innovation
  • Thailand 4.0: national strategy to move from low-value production to innovation and technology-driven growth.
  • Eastern Economic Corridor (EEC): zone in Chonburi, Rayong and Chachoengsao with incentives for advanced industries such as next-generation automotive, smart electronics and robotics.
  • Economic lens: higher productivity and value added. Challenges: SME readiness, skills and capital. Policy pushes automation and digital upgrading in automotive and electronics.

BMW Group (digital twins)

  • Built virtual copies of factories to plan production before building. Newer plants such as Debrecen, Hungary use cameras and sensors on the line for quality checks.
  • Mistakes are found on screen, which is cheaper than changing a real line. Economic lens: higher upfront software cost, lower cost of errors and faster launches.

Schneider Electric, Le Vaudreuil (France)

  • A connected plant using the company's own EcoStruxure systems; recognized by the World Economic Forum as a leading factory.
  • Energy, assets and production are monitored in one place, supporting faster decisions. Economic lens: a vendor uses its own factory as a showcase, which builds trust with customers.

Thai manufacturers getting started

  • Automotive and electronics firms often pilot machine monitoring, energy tracking and quality inspection.
  • What helps: Thailand 4.0 and EEC programs, training and government incentives (the deck notes: check current BOI measures). Gains are larger when SMEs can access finance, skills and shared testbeds.

7. Challenges and risks

Upfront cost

Sensors, software and integration need capital before returns arrive.

Skills gap

Firms need data, IT and automation skills.

Cybersecurity

Connected machines create new attack points.

Data integration

Old machines and separate systems are hard to connect.

SME finance

Small firms struggle to fund and scale pilots.

Workforce change

Roles shift, so reskilling is needed.

Key takeaways

  • Smart industry connects machines, data and people, building on four industrial revolutions.
  • Digital transformation changes productivity, cost structure, skills and business models.
  • Smart manufacturing connects, analyzes and acts, and works best when started with a pilot.
  • Benefits (productivity, lower cost, quality, flexibility) come against high upfront investment.
  • Discussion: who gains and who loses when routine factory jobs are automated? Why might firms in emerging economies adopt smart technology more slowly? What should a small manufacturer weigh? How should governments help SMEs?

B2C and C2B Business

Source: B2C and C2B Business (9 slides), Economics of the Digital Economy. Who creates the value, and who pays for it?

Guiding question: Lecture's guiding question: who creates the value, and who pays for it? Subtitle: who starts the transaction in a digital market?

Learning goals: Explain what B2C and C2B mean and how value flows; Analyze real digital examples and the economic forces behind them; Evaluate who gains, who bears risk and who holds pricing power.

B2C: businesses sell to consumers

Business → Consumer. A firm builds a product or service and sells it to individual buyers through an app, website or marketplace.

Digital examples

  • Online retail: Shopee, Lazada, Amazon
  • Streaming: Netflix, Spotify
  • Ride-hailing and delivery: Grab
  • Digital banking and e-wallets

Economic logic

  • Near-zero marginal cost for digital goods
  • Price transparency raises competition
  • Data enables personalized offers (AI)

C2B: consumers sell to businesses

Consumer → Business. Individuals supply something a business wants: skills, content, data, attention or a price they will accept.

Digital examples

  • Freelance platforms: Upwork, Fiverr, Fastwork
  • Stock photo and music contributors (Shutterstock)
  • Influencers paid by brands (TikTok Shop)
  • Reverse auctions: the buyer names a price (Sotheby's, eBay, as listed in the deck)

Economic logic

  • Platforms cut the cost of finding individual sellers
  • Consumers become producers ("prosumers")
  • Flexible, often gig-based supply

Four ideas that explain both models

Transaction costs

Platforms lower the cost of searching, negotiating and paying, so small trades become worthwhile.

Network effects

More users attract more buyers or sellers, which favors a few large platforms.

Information asymmetry

Ratings, reviews and escrow narrow the gap in what each side knows about quality.

Two-sided markets

Platforms serve two groups and often subsidize one side, such as free access for consumers.

Last-minute cheat sheet

One page.

1 · Banking & currency

  • Digital banking = channel; digital currency = form of money.
  • Banking: internet / mobile / digital-only. PromptPay 2017. Benefits: cost, 24/7, inclusion. Risks: fraud, cyber, divide, privacy.
  • Types: Crypto (network; supply and demand; BTC 2009, cap 21M) · Stablecoin (company; reserves; USDT/USDC) · CBDC (central bank; legal tender; e-CNY, Sand Dollar).
  • 5 steps: Wallet → Transaction → Validation → Ledger → Settlement.
  • Stablecoins: fiat / crypto / algorithmic (TerraUSD 2022). Regulation: EU MiCA, US GENIUS Act.
  • Crypto risks: volatility, custody (Mt. Gox 2014, FTX 2022), scams, regulation, energy.
  • Retail CBDC = public; wholesale = banks. BoT: Inthanon, mBridge.

2 · Smart industry

  • Industry 1.0 mechanization (1700s) · 2.0 mass production (1900s) · 3.0 automation (1970s) · 4.0 IoT/data/AI (2010s+).
  • Tech: IoT, analytics, AI/ML, cloud/edge, digital twin + 3D print, robots/cobots.
  • Connect → Analyze → Act. Roadmap: goals → pilot → connect → analyze → scale → people.
  • Amberg (1989): 1 product/sec, 75% automated, 99.9988% quality, 350 changeovers/day, ~1,200 products, ~8× output, ~40% gains from employee ideas.
  • Haier COSMOPlat: users co-design. Thailand 4.0 + EEC (Chonburi, Rayong, Chachoengsao). BMW digital twins (Debrecen). Schneider Le Vaudreuil (EcoStruxure, WEF).
  • Use cases: predictive maintenance, mass customization, supply chain visibility, AI inspection, digital twin, cobots.
  • Challenges: upfront cost, skills, cyber, data integration, SME finance, workforce.

3 · E-commerce

  • E-commerce = transaction; e-business = whole operation.
  • Models: B2C, B2B, C2C, D2C, subscription, marketplace, dropshipping.
  • Journey: Discover → Compare → Checkout → Fulfil → Deliver → Support. Last mile = costliest.
  • Card flow: Customer → Merchant → Gateway/PSP → Acquirer → Issuer.
  • Economics: search costs fall · long tail · low marginal cost · price transparency. Platforms: two-sided, network effects, winner-take-most.
  • Metrics: conversion, AOV, CAC, CLV, cart abandonment, ROAS.
  • SEA 2023 GMV US$114.6B; 2025: ID 57.7 · TH 35.5 · PH 21.1 · VN 20.4 · MY 17.0 · SG 5.9 (US$B). Shopee 49–58% everywhere; TikTok Shop rising; Lazada falling (TH 30 → 18%).
  • Regulation: PDPA, PCI DSS, VAT/digital service tax, consumer protection. Trends: AI personalization, zero-commission, AI chatbots, mobile-first.

4 · Crumbl & marketing

  • Bakery as tech startup: A/B tested recipes · ML inventory (sales, weather, calendar, ratings) · app + iPad kiosks · custom logistics (+DoorDash) · weekly drops → FOMO → TikTok/Instagram pink boxes.
  • Five strategies P-V-C-I-S: AI Personalization (Netflix, Spotify Wrapped) · short-form Video + creators (Duolingo, Glossier) · Community (GoPro, LEGO Ideas; ladder buyers → fans → advocates → co-creators) · Immersive (IKEA Place, Sephora Virtual Artist) · Social commerce (Shopee Live, TikTok Shop, LINE OA).
  • Start: segment+recommend · pilot 5 micro-creators · UGC hashtag · quiz/preview · one live-stream sale.

5 · B2C & C2B

  • B2C: business → consumer; consumer pays; seller sets price; mass market; risk = price competition, low switching costs (Netflix).
  • C2B: consumer → business; business pays; individual/bidding sets price; many small suppliers; risk = quality control, unstable income (Fiverr, Upwork, Shutterstock, influencers).
  • Prosumers, gig supply. Four ideas: transaction costs · network effects · information asymmetry · two-sided markets.
  • Ratings, reviews, escrow narrow the information gap. One person can be B2C buyer and C2B seller.
  • Always ask: who creates the value, and who pays for it?
